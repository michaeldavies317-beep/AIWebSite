Parent guide

AI, homework and chatbots

For parents of 10 to 13 year olds. This is the age when children start to use AI on their own, often for homework. Here's how to help them use it well, honestly and safely.

  • Helping versus cheating: a simple line
  • Age limits on the popular chatbots
  • Privacy settings, step by step
  • Chatbot "friends" and companion apps

1

What schools usually allow, and how to ask yours

Schools in the UK are writing their own AI rules, so they differ. Many allow AI to explain, quiz and give feedback, but not to write work that's handed in as the child's own.

Ask your child's school (an email to the class teacher or head of year is fine):

Hello [name], could you tell me the school's guidance on pupils using AI tools such as ChatGPT for homework? In particular, what's allowed, what isn't, and whether pupils should say when they've used it. Thank you, [your name], parent of [child] in [class].

2

Helping versus cheating: a simple line

A rule children understand: AI can be your tutor, not your ghostwriter.

Good use (a tutor)Not OK (a ghostwriter)
"Explain fractions another way""Do my maths homework"
"Quiz me on the Tudors""Write my essay on the Tudors"
"What could make my story more exciting?""Rewrite my story so it's better"
"Check my spelling and tell me what I got wrong""Fix everything and give it back"

A good test: could you explain this to your teacher without the AI? If yes, it helped you learn.

I'm [age] and I'm stuck on my homework about [topic]. Don't give me the answer. Explain it step by step and ask me questions to help me work it out myself.

3

Age limits on the popular chatbots

Most AI apps set a minimum age in their rules. At the time of writing:

  • ChatGPT: 13 and over, and under-18s need a parent's permission. It has parental controls that let you link to your teen's account.
  • Gemini: Google lets teens use it, and in some countries younger children can use it through a Family Link account a parent manages.
  • Claude: 18 and over.
  • Chatbots inside social apps (for example Snapchat's My AI or Meta AI in WhatsApp and Instagram): these follow the app's own age limit, usually 13.

Age limits are there for good reasons, but many children still find their way onto these apps. It's better to know what they use and talk about it than to discover it later.

Rules change often. Check each app's own help pages before deciding.

4

Privacy settings, step by step

Sit down together and go through these. It takes 15 minutes and is a good chance to talk about why.

  1. Use a family account where you can, for example a child account in Google Family Link or Apple Family Sharing, so you can see which apps are installed.
  2. Switch off "improve the model" or similar in the app's data or privacy settings.
  3. Check memory: look at what the app has remembered about your child, and delete anything personal.
  4. Turn on parental controls where the app offers them. ChatGPT, for example, lets you link accounts, set quiet hours and turn off features like memory.
  5. Agree the "never share" list: full name, address, school, passwords, photos of themselves or friends.
5

Chatbot "friends" and companion apps

Some apps let children chat with AI characters that act like friends, sometimes very convincingly. Children can find them comforting, especially if they're lonely or anxious. They can also encourage too much time online, or say things that aren't appropriate.

  • Ask, don't ban first: "Have you ever chatted to an AI character? What was it like?"
  • Explain the difference: "It's designed to keep you chatting. It doesn't really know you or care about you the way your friends and family do."
  • Agree limits: no companion apps for now, or only ones you've looked at together.
  • Watch for changes: secrecy, upset when they can't use it, or pulling away from real friends are signs to talk.
6

Fake pictures and videos, explained kindly

AI can make photos, voices and videos that look real. Children will see them, and some may be tempted to make them.

"Not everything you see online is real, even if it looks it. If a picture or video makes you feel shocked, scared or angry, stop and check before you share it."
  • Checking: who posted it? Is a trusted news site reporting it? Does anything look odd, like hands, text or shadows?
  • Making: it's never OK to make a fake picture or video of a real person without permission, even as a joke. It can really hurt people, and some fakes are against the law.
  • If they're targeted: reassure them it's not their fault, save the evidence, report it on the app, and tell the school. Childline's Report Remove tool helps under-18s get images taken down.
