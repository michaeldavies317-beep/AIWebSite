Parent guide

First steps with AI

A calm, practical guide for parents of children under 10. AI is already in your home, in smart speakers, tablets and toys. Here's how to make it a safe, fun part of family life.

  • Kid-friendly apps, and which to wait on
  • Fun things to make together
  • Three conversation starters

1

What AI is, in words they'll get

Young children don't need the technical details. They need a simple, honest picture they can come back to.

"AI is a computer program that has read lots and lots of words. When you ask it something, it guesses a good answer. It's clever, but it isn't a person, it doesn't have feelings, and sometimes it gets things wrong."

Then try a little game: ask a smart speaker or chatbot a silly question together ("What colour is Tuesday?") and talk about the answer. Did it really know? Children learn quickly that AI is a tool, not a friend or a grown-up.

2

Smart speakers and tablets: the safe settings

  • Amazon Alexa: in the Alexa app, set up Amazon Kids for your child's profile. It filters explicit songs and answers, and adds kid-friendly responses. Turn off voice purchasing, or add a PIN, under Settings.
  • Google Nest or Home: in the Google Home app, open Digital Wellbeing to filter explicit content and set "downtime" hours. Turn on Voice Match so it knows who's speaking.
  • iPad or iPhone: use Screen Time with Family Sharing to choose which apps your child can use and for how long. Set Ask to Buy so they can't download apps without you.
  • Android tablets: use the free Google Family Link app to approve apps and set daily limits.

Step-by-step instructions for each device are in our Family set-up guide.

3

Kid-friendly apps, and which to wait on

Most general chatbots, such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, are designed for teenagers or adults, and their rules say so. For under 10s, the simplest approach is:

  • Use AI together, on your device, rather than giving them their own chatbot account.
  • Choose apps made for children, with clear age ratings in the App Store or Google Play, and look for a "made for kids" or "teacher approved" label.
  • Wait on chatbot "friend" or companion apps, apps with open chat with strangers, and anything that asks for a photo of your child.

Not sure about an app? Use the "is this app OK?" checklist in the bundle, or email us.

4

Fun things to make together

The best way to teach AI is to enjoy it together, with you in charge of the keyboard.

  • Story time: your child picks the hero, the place and a problem; AI writes a short story; you read it aloud. Then ask "How would you change the ending?"
  • Picture ideas: describe a made-up animal together and ask AI to draw it. Talk about how the computer "imagined" it.
  • Quiz master: ask AI for five easy questions about their favourite topic: dinosaurs, space, football.
  • Why, why, why: when they ask a question you can't answer, look it up together and check the answer in a book or a trusted website.

Write a short, gentle bedtime story for a [age]-year-old about [hero] who visits [place] and solves [problem]. Under 300 words, with a happy ending and no scary parts.

5

Three conversation starters

  • "If you could ask a robot any question, what would it be?"
  • "Do you think the computer really knows the answer, or is it guessing?"
  • "What should we never tell a computer?" (Your name, school, address, passwords, and photos of you.)

Our printable conversation cards in the bundle have 16 more, sorted by age.

6

When to step in, and how to stay calm

Most of the time, AI with young children is harmless fun. Step in if your child:

  • has seen or heard something that upset or confused them,
  • has shared personal information or a photo,
  • talks about an app as if it were a real friend who keeps secrets.

Stay calm and curious: "Thanks for telling me. Show me what happened." Praise them for telling you, so they keep telling you. Then change the setting, block the app, or report it, and move on.

If you're ever worried about a child's safety online, the NSPCC helpline (0808 800 5000) and the CEOP reporting service from the National Crime Agency can help.
